\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-based hallucination detectors verify the claims of a large language
model (LLM) against retrieved evidence, and every document they read adds the
cost of natural-language-inference (NLI) scoring. Bayesian sequential
estimation (BSE) reduces this cost by reading documents one at a time and
stopping once further evidence is not worth its cost. Both its decisions and
its stopping rest on a likelihood ratio that BSE obtains by dividing two
histograms of entailment scores. We replace this ratio, and nothing else,
with a directly estimated density ratio (uLSIF). We also show that BSE's
published one-step look-ahead never reads a document at its own cost
settings, and derive a backward-induction stopping rule that any ratio
estimator can use. In a preregistered test on HELM (3,736 sentences from
seven LLMs), the direct ratio's cost--quality curve lies above BSE's at both
published cost settings (area gap $+0.043$ and $+0.037$ balanced average
precision). At BSE's detection quality it needs 70\% fewer NLI calls, and it
lies above a fixed-$k$ maximum-entailment baseline that BSE does not beat. A
retriever-aware variant that adds contradiction and retrieval rank is not
supported.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models produce fluent text that is not always true
\citep{ji2023survey}. A widely used way to detect such hallucinations is to
decompose a response into claims, retrieve evidence for each claim, and score
claim--evidence pairs with an NLI model
\citep{min-etal-2023-factscore,wang-etal-2024-factcheck}. The cost of these
detectors grows with every document they read. This matters most in deployed
assistants that verify responses before they reach users, such as
customer-service systems in enterprises, where each retrieved and scored
document adds compute and latency to every response.

\citet{wang-etal-2023-hallucination} cast detection as Bayesian sequential
estimation (BSE). Documents are read in retrieval order. After each one, the
probability that the claim is factual is updated with a likelihood ratio, and
a Bayes-risk rule decides whether another document is worth reading
\citep{wald1945sequential}. The quality of the final decision and the number
of documents read therefore both depend on how well this ratio is estimated.
BSE estimates it by counting two 10-bin histograms of entailment scores, one
for factual and one for hallucinated claims, and dividing them. The
density-ratio literature advises against exactly this route: estimating two
densities is harder than estimating their ratio, and dividing them amplifies
the errors of both \citep{sugiyama2012density}.

This paper asks whether estimating the ratio \emph{directly} makes sequential
hallucination detection cheaper and better when everything else is held
fixed. We study three goals: reaching BSE's detection quality with fewer NLI
calls (\textbf{G1}), detecting hallucinations better at BSE's cost
(\textbf{G2}), and producing better-calibrated probabilities (\textbf{G3}).
Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A drop-in replacement of BSE's likelihood ratio by direct density-ratio
estimation (uLSIF; \citealp{kanamori2009ulsif}), with the documents, prior,
update, stopping rule and aggregation unchanged (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item An analysis of BSE's stopping rule. We prove that the published one-step
look-ahead reads no document whenever no single document can change the
decision, which is the case at BSE's own costs, and we give a
backward-induction rule whose look-ahead any ratio estimator can evaluate
from its training sample (\S\ref{sec:stopping}).
\item A preregistered confirmatory test on a dataset that none of our
detectors had touched. The direct ratio's cost--quality curve lies above
BSE's and above a fixed-$k$ baseline (\S\ref{sec:helm}).
\item A full account of what did not work, including a retriever-aware
multivariate ratio that does not beat its own histogram control
(\S\ref{sec:negative}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Hallucination detection.} Detectors differ in the evidence they
use. SelfCheckGPT compares a response with stochastic samples from the same
model \citep{manakul-etal-2023-selfcheckgpt}. FActScore and Factcheck-GPT
decompose a response into atomic claims and verify each against retrieved
documents \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore,wang-etal-2024-factcheck}, and
long-form evaluators extend this idea \citep{wei2024longform}. MIND trains a
detector on the model's internal states \citep{su-etal-2024-unsupervised}.
Among retrieval-based detectors, BSE \citep{wang-etal-2023-hallucination} is,
to our knowledge, the only one that decides adaptively how many documents to
read. We change its evidence model and its stopping computation.

\paragraph{Sequential decisions.} Sequential analysis
\citep{wald1945sequential} trades the cost of further observations against
the risk of a wrong decision. The Bayes-optimal stopping rule is obtained by
backward induction, which BSE approximates by a one-step look-ahead
\citep{cardillo1968suboptimal}. Because the state of our problem is a single
posterior probability, we solve the backward induction exactly on a grid.

\paragraph{Density-ratio estimation.} Estimating a ratio of densities
directly, without estimating either density, underlies KLIEP
\citep{sugiyama2007kliep}, uLSIF \citep{kanamori2009ulsif}, its relative
variant RuLSIF \citep{yamada2011rulsif} and probabilistic-classification
estimators \citep{bickel2009discriminative}. All are instances of
density-ratio matching under a Bregman divergence
\citep{sugiyama2012bregman,sugiyama2012density}, and they are used for
covariate-shift adaptation, two-sample testing and outlier detection. We use
them as the evidence model of a sequential hallucination detector.

\section{Background: Bayesian Sequential Estimation}
\label{sec:bse}

\paragraph{Notation.} A response is split into claims. For a claim $c$, a
retriever returns documents $d_1, \dots, d_K$ in rank order. An NLI model maps
each claim--document pair to features $x_n = \phi(c, d_n)$. In BSE,
$x_n = e_n \in [0, 100]$ is the largest entailment probability, in percent,
over 400-word spans of $d_n$. Let $\theta = 1$ if $c$ is factual and
$\theta = 0$ if it is hallucinated, let $\pf$ and $\ph$ be the densities of
$x$ under the two classes, and let $r(x) = \pf(x)/\ph(x)$ be their likelihood
ratio.

\paragraph{Posterior update.} BSE starts from the prior $\pi_0 = 0.5$ and,
treating documents as conditionally independent given $\theta$, updates the
probability that $c$ is factual after each document:
\begin{equation}
\pi_n = \frac{\pi_{n-1}\, r(x_n)}{\pi_{n-1}\, r(x_n) + 1 - \pi_{n-1}}.
\label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
BSE estimates $r$ by counting the entailment scores of $N_f$ factual and
$N_h$ hallucinated training pairs in ten bins $b(e)$ and dividing the two
Laplace-smoothed bin frequencies,
$r_{\mathrm{BSE}}(e) = \hat\pf(b(e)) / \hat\ph(b(e))$, where
$\hat\pf(b) = (n_f(b) + 1)/(N_f + 10)$ and $\hat\ph$ is defined likewise.

\paragraph{Decision and stopping.} Declaring a hallucinated claim factual
costs $\CM$ (a miss), and declaring a factual claim hallucinated costs $\CFA$
(a false alarm). Stopping at posterior $\pi$ therefore has Bayes risk
$R(\pi) = \min\{(1 - \pi)\CM,\ \pi\CFA\}$, attained by declaring the claim
factual iff $\pi > \tau = \CM/(\CM + \CFA)$. Each document costs $\Cr$ to
read. BSE reads document $d_{n+1}$ iff stopping is riskier than reading one
more document and then stopping:
\begin{equation}
R(\pi_n) > \Cr + \E_{x \sim q_{\pi_n}}\bigl[R(\pi_{n+1}(x))\bigr],
\label{eq:onestep}
\end{equation}
where $q_\pi = \pi \pf + (1 - \pi) \ph$ is the predictive density of the next
document's features. A sentence's probability is the minimum over its claims,
and the sentence is labelled factual iff this minimum exceeds $\tau$. BSE was
evaluated at $(\CM, \CFA) = (14, 24)$ and $(28, 96)$, with $\Cr = 1$.

\section{Direct Density-Ratio Evidence}
\label{sec:method}

Eq.~\ref{eq:update} uses the two class-conditional densities only through
their ratio. We therefore estimate $r$ directly and leave every other part of
BSE unchanged.

\paragraph{Estimator.} We use unconstrained least-squares importance fitting
(uLSIF; \citealp{kanamori2009ulsif}). Let $x^f_1, \dots, x^f_{n_f}$ be the
features of documents retrieved for factual training claims and
$x^h_1, \dots, x^h_{n_h}$ those of documents for hallucinated claims, all
standardised on the pooled sample. The ratio is modelled as a non-negative
combination of $L$ Gaussian kernels $k_l(x) = \exp(-\lVert x - c_l\rVert^2 /
2\sigma^2)$, with centres $c_l$ drawn from the factual sample, and fitted by
minimising the squared error $\tfrac12 \E_{\ph}[(\hat r - r)^2]$. Up to a
constant this error equals $\tfrac12 \E_{\ph}[\hat r^2] - \E_{\pf}[\hat r]$, so
with a ridge penalty the fit is a quadratic program with a closed-form
solution:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\hat r(x) &= \textstyle\sum_{l=1}^{L} \hat\alpha_l\, k_l(x), \\
\hat\alpha &= \max\bigl\{0,\ (\hat H + \lambda I)^{-1} \hat h\bigr\},
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ulsif}
\end{equation}
where $\hat H_{ll'} = \frac{1}{n_h}\sum_{j} k_l(x^h_j)\, k_{l'}(x^h_j)$ and
$\hat h_l = \frac{1}{n_f}\sum_{i} k_l(x^f_i)$ are the empirical second and
first moments of the kernels, and the elementwise maximum sets negative
weights to zero.

\paragraph{Model selection and normalisation.} The kernel width $\sigma$ and
the penalty $\lambda$ minimise the held-out value of the same squared-error
criterion, over five folds grouped by claim, so that no detection metric
enters the selection (Appendix~\ref{app:fit}). A likelihood ratio averages
one under its denominator, $\E_{\ph}[r] = 1$, but the fitted $\hat r$ need
not, and any offset would bias every update in Eq.~\ref{eq:update}. We
therefore divide $\hat r$ by its mean over the hallucinated training
documents, clip it to $[10^{-6}, 10^{6}]$, and use the result $\tilde r$ in
place of $r$.

\paragraph{Variants.} \textbf{DDRE-1D} fits $\tilde r(e)$ on exactly BSE's
training pairs, so that against BSE it changes only how the ratio is
estimated. Figure~\ref{fig:ratio} compares the two fitted ratios. The
retriever-aware variant \textbf{DDRE-M} uses $x = (e, k, \log \mathrm{rank})$,
adding the largest contradiction probability $k$ (from the same NLI pass) and
the document's retrieval rank. Its control, \textbf{NB-M}, multiplies
per-feature 10-bin histograms on the same features (Naive Bayes).

\section{Stopping with an Arbitrary Ratio Estimator}
\label{sec:stopping}

Eq.~\ref{eq:onestep} requires an expectation under the predictive density
$q_\pi$, which a histogram supplies through its bin frequencies. A direct
ratio estimator supplies no densities. The following identity shows that it
does not need them.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:identity}
For any integrable function $g$ and any $\pi \in [0, 1]$,
\begin{equation}
\E_{x \sim q_\pi}[g(x)] = \E_{x \sim \ph}\bigl[(\pi\, r(x) + 1 - \pi)\, g(x)\bigr].
\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
In particular, writing $\pi'(x)$ for the posterior after observing $x$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&(\pi\, r(x) + 1 - \pi)\, R(\pi'(x)) \\
&\quad= \min\bigl\{(1 - \pi)\CM,\ \pi\, r(x)\, \CFA\bigr\}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:closed}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\pf = r\, \ph$, we have $q_\pi = (\pi r + 1 - \pi)\, \ph$, which gives
Eq.~\ref{eq:identity}. By Eq.~\ref{eq:update},
$1 - \pi'(x) = (1 - \pi)/(\pi r(x) + 1 - \pi)$ and
$\pi'(x) = \pi r(x)/(\pi r(x) + 1 - \pi)$; substituting both into
$R$ gives Eq.~\ref{eq:closed}.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:identity} lets any ratio estimator evaluate the
look-ahead by averaging over its own hallucinated training sample: the
support $\mathcal{S} = \{\tilde r(x^h_j)\}_{j=1}^{n_h}$ replaces the unknown
density $\ph$. With BSE's histogram, the same computation reproduces
Eq.~\ref{eq:onestep} exactly.

\paragraph{The one-step rule can be inert.} The one-step rule values a document
only through its chance of changing the decision.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:inert}
Suppose the current decision is ``factual'' ($\pi > \tau$) and no single
document can reverse it, that is, $\pi'(x) > \tau$ for every $x$. Then
$\E_{q_\pi}[R(\pi'(x))] = R(\pi)$, so Eq.~\ref{eq:onestep} holds for no
$\Cr > 0$, and the rule reads no document.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $\{\pi > \tau\}$ the risk is linear, $R(\pi) = (1 - \pi)\CM$. The
posterior is a martingale under the predictive density: by
Eq.~\ref{eq:identity}, $\E_{q_\pi}[\pi'(x)] = \E_{\ph}[\pi r(x)] = \pi$.
Hence $\E_{q_\pi}[R(\pi'(x))] = (1 - \E_{q_\pi}[\pi'(x)])\,\CM = R(\pi)$.
\end{proof}

At BSE's main setting, $(\CM, \CFA) = (28, 96)$, the threshold is
$\tau = 28/124 = 0.226$, and the prior $\pi_0 = 0.5$ already lies on the
factual side. Reversing the decision in one step requires a log ratio below
$\operatorname{logit}(\tau) = -1.23$, but the most negative bin of BSE's
histogram has $\log r = -0.69$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:inert}, the published
rule therefore reads no document at this setting, for any $\Cr > 0$; two
independent implementations confirm this. BSE's released code instead
evaluates $R$ at the \emph{unweighted mean} of the ten possible next
posteriors. This heuristic continues while $\pi \in (0.204, 0.769)$, which is
why the released system reads documents at all.

\paragraph{Backward induction.} The optimal rule looks ahead over all remaining
documents. With $n$ documents read and at most $K$ available, the minimal
expected cost $V_n$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
V_n(\pi) = \min\bigl\{R(\pi),\ \Cr + \E_{q_{\pi}}[V_{n+1}(\pi'(x))]\bigr\},
\label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
with $V_K = R$. \citet{wang-etal-2023-hallucination} describe this solution
but approximate it, because induction over discrete features grows
exponentially with $K$. Our state is the scalar posterior, so we solve
Eq.~\ref{eq:bellman} on a fine logit grid and evaluate every expectation with
Eq.~\ref{eq:identity}. For $K = 1$ the rule coincides with
Eq.~\ref{eq:onestep}. For BSE at $(28, 96)$, $\Cr = 1$ and $K = 10$, it reads
while $\pi \in (0.034, 0.813)$. Every method in our comparisons is stopped by
this rule (Algorithm~\ref{alg:detect}), so the methods differ only in the
ratio.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Sequential detection of one claim}
\label{alg:detect}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require claim $c$; documents $d_1, \dots, d_K$ in retrieval order; ratio $\tilde r$ and support $\mathcal{S}$; costs $\CM$, $\CFA$, $\Cr$
\Ensure probability $\pi$ that $c$ is factual
\State $V_K \gets R$ \Comment{offline, once per $K$}
\For{$n = K-1, \dots, 0$}
  \State $V_n \gets$ Eq.~\ref{eq:bellman}, averaging over $\mathcal{S}$ by Eq.~\ref{eq:identity}
\EndFor
\State $\pi \gets \pi_0$
\For{$n = 0, \dots, K-1$}
  \If{$V_n(\pi) = R(\pi)$} \Comment{stopping is optimal}
    \State \textbf{break}
  \EndIf
  \State $x \gets \phi(c, d_{n+1})$ \Comment{NLI on each span}
  \State $\pi \gets \pi\, \tilde r(x) / \bigl(\pi\, \tilde r(x) + 1 - \pi\bigr)$
\EndFor
\State \Return $\pi$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Methods.} We compare BSE with DDRE-1D.
Both are fitted on BSE's released training pairs
\citep{wang-etal-2023-hallucination}, 199 per class, and both are stopped by
backward induction. As references we report \emph{BSE-official}, which uses
the released code's look-ahead, and \emph{fixed-$k$}, BSE's own ``Maximum''
baseline, which reads the first $k$ documents and scores a claim by their
largest entailment. NB-M and DDRE-M are fitted on an entity-disjoint split of
FActScore \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore}: 7,382 facts with ten
BM25-retrieved Wikipedia passages each. NLI features come from BSE's
DeBERTa-v3 checkpoint \citep{he2023debertav3,laurer2024less} in float16 with
BSE's segmentation; before every scoring run, the training pairs must
reproduce earlier scores bit for bit.

\paragraph{Data.} Table~\ref{tab:data} lists the evaluation sets. Five were
used during development and are therefore \emph{exploratory}: the SelfCheckGPT
held-out split with BSE's saved web pages
\citep{manakul-etal-2023-selfcheckgpt}, the FActScore test entities with BM25
Wikipedia evidence, Factcheck-Bench \citep{wang-etal-2024-factcheck}, ExpertQA
\citep{malaviya-etal-2024-expertqa} and BingCheck \citep{li-etal-2024-self}.
The confirmatory set is HELM \citep{su-etal-2024-unsupervised}: sentences
written by seven LLMs to continue Wikipedia articles, each labelled by two
annotators. Each HELM sentence is a single claim, and its evidence is the ten
highest-ranked BM25 passages from a 2023 Wikipedia snapshot, retrieved with
the article title and the sentence as the query.

\paragraph{Quality and cost.} We measure detection by balanced average
precision, $\mathrm{bAP} = \tfrac12(\mathrm{AP}_{\mathrm{fact}} +
\mathrm{AP}_{\mathrm{hal}})$, where $\mathrm{AP}_{\mathrm{fact}}$ ranks
sentences by their probability $P$ of being factual and
$\mathrm{AP}_{\mathrm{hal}}$ by $1 - P$. We prefer AP to the trapezoidal
PR-AUC of BSE's code, which rewards tied scores: a constant score obtains
$0.75$ balanced trapezoidal PR-AUC but $0.5$ bAP, and histogram posteriors are
heavily tied. Cost is the number of NLI calls per sentence.

\paragraph{Cost--quality curves.} Each method is run at every combination of
$K' \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ documents and $\Cr \in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1\}$, at
both of BSE's cost settings. Method $m$ thus yields operating points
$(\kappa_s, q_s)$ of mean NLI calls and bAP, and its curve is the step
envelope $Q_m(\kappa) = \max\{q_s : \kappa_s \le \kappa\}$, the best quality
available within a cost $\kappa$. When method $m$ is
compared with a baseline $b$, the primary endpoint is the area gap over their
shared cost range $[\kappa_{\mathrm{lo}}, \kappa_{\mathrm{hi}}]$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{AUC}} = \frac{1}{\kappa_{\mathrm{hi}} - \kappa_{\mathrm{lo}}}
  \int_{\kappa_{\mathrm{lo}}}^{\kappa_{\mathrm{hi}}} \bigl(Q_m(\kappa) - Q_b(\kappa)\bigr)\, d\kappa.
\label{eq:auc}
\end{equation}
For G1 we report the NLI calls saved at the baseline's operating quality,
and for G2 the bAP gained at its operating cost, both read off the envelopes.
The operating point is $K' = K$ and $\Cr = 1$.

\paragraph{Statistics and preregistration.} Intervals come from a paired
cluster bootstrap with 10,000 replicates, in which every method is evaluated
on the same resampled clusters. The HELM hypotheses, endpoints and decision
rule were merged as a preregistration \citep{nosek2018preregistration} before
any retrieval, NLI scoring or detector touched HELM. The two hypotheses, H1
(DDRE-1D above BSE) and H2 (DDRE-1D above fixed-$k$), are each tested at both
cost settings with one-sided bootstrap $p$-values and Holm's correction
\citep{holm1979simple}. A test is supported if Holm rejects and the 5th
percentile of $\Delta_{\mathrm{AUC}}$ is positive.

\end{table*}

Table~\ref{tab:operating} reports every method at the operating point
itself. There, DDRE-1D reads slightly more than BSE (5.87 vs.\ 5.46 NLI calls
at $(28, 96)$) for a slightly higher bAP. Its advantage lies in reaching any given quality more cheaply along the
curve, not in reading fewer documents at one fixed setting. Its Brier score is
lower than BSE's (\textbf{G3}), although not lower than BSE-official's, which
reads fewer documents and stays closer to the prior.

\paragraph{Robustness.} H1 holds in every preregistered sensitivity analysis:
when the 22\% of passages that come from the source article are removed
($\Delta_{\mathrm{AUC}} = +0.038$ and $+0.032$), on the six generators
described by HELM's authors ($+0.043$ and $+0.037$), and for each of the seven
generators separately ($+0.026$ to $+0.051$). H2 is narrower: at $(28, 96)$
it is not supported on the six-generator subset ($+0.005$), and two of the
seven generators are slightly negative there (Appendix~\ref{app:helm}).

\subsection{What Did Not Work}
\label{sec:negative}

\paragraph{Retriever-aware features.} Adding contradiction and retrieval rank
helps the histogram estimator: NB-M has the highest bAP on HELM
(Table~\ref{tab:operating}). It does not help uLSIF. DDRE-M lies below its
histogram control NB-M on HELM ($\Delta_{\mathrm{AUC}} = -0.024$ and $-0.033$)
and on most exploratory datasets, so we do not support the retriever-aware
variant.

\paragraph{BSE and the simple baseline.} BSE's envelope lies below fixed-$k$
maximum entailment on HELM ($-0.041$ and $-0.028$) and on four of the five
exploratory datasets. Claims about the efficiency of sequential detectors
should be made against this baseline.

\paragraph{Earlier configurations.} Before adopting the single-factor design
above, we evaluated a one-dimensional ratio whose stopping band and kernel
width were tuned over 640 validation configurations, and a multivariate ratio
run under BSE's stopping band. Both differed from BSE in more than the ratio.
Their gains did not transfer across datasets, and the second was not
confirmed by an earlier preregistered test on ExpertQA and BingCheck
(Appendix~\ref{app:history}).

\section{Conclusion}

Estimating BSE's likelihood ratio directly, with everything else held fixed,
moves its cost--quality curve upward. Under a preregistered test, the direct
ratio reaches BSE's detection quality with 70\% fewer NLI calls and lies above
a simple fixed-$k$ baseline that BSE does not beat. The quality gains are
modest, about 0.04 bAP across the curve, and they appear along the curve
rather than at a single operating point; evaluations of sequential detectors
should therefore report curves and include the fixed-$k$ baseline. BSE's
published one-step look-ahead is inert at its own costs, and the
backward-induction rule we derive applies to any ratio estimator.

For deployed assistants such as customer-service systems, where NLI scoring of
retrieved evidence dominates the cost of verifying a response, these savings
suggest a proportional reduction in verification compute. We did not test
that setting; an evaluation on enterprise data with measured latency is the
natural next step.

\section*{Limitations}

The effects are modest, and H2 at $(28, 96)$ is narrow. Only HELM is
confirmatory; the other five datasets were used during development. On HELM we
retrieved evidence ourselves from a Wikipedia snapshot instead of by live web
search, and sentences were not decomposed into claims. HELM's annotators also
consulted Google, the treatment of ``unverifiable'' sentences in its binary
labels is undocumented, its released counts differ from those in its paper,
and it states no licence; we contacted its authors on 17 October 2026. BSE and
DDRE-1D are fitted on BSE's 398 training pairs, which are scored on whole
premises, whereas test documents are scored as the maximum over spans; both
methods share this mismatch. We count cost in NLI calls and did not measure
wall-clock time. The estimator's own cost, a histogram lookup or a kernel sum
over 100 centres, is small next to NLI scoring, but we have not measured it.

\section*{Ethics Statement}

All datasets are public research releases, used under their terms and not
redistributed; for releases without a stated licence we contacted the authors.
The detectors flag possibly false claims and do not alter model outputs. A
detector of modest accuracy can miss hallucinations or flag correct
statements, so in deployment it should support, not replace, human review of
consequential responses.

\section{BSE: Paper and Released Code}
\label{app:deviations}

BSE's released code differs from the paper in several details. We follow the
code, which produced the published numbers; our reproduction matches the
published results at $(\CM, \CFA) = (28, 96)$, for example a nonfactual PR-AUC
of 0.860 against 0.865 and 6.47 against 6.22 documents per sentence. In the
code, spans are 400 \emph{words} with a stride of 300, rather than tokens;
logits are divided by a temperature of 5 before the softmax; the three-class
NLI output is used through its entailment probability; training pairs and test
documents are discretised by two different rules; the training pairs (199 per
class, not 200) are scored on the whole premise, whereas test documents are
scored as the maximum over spans; and the look-ahead averages the ten possible
next posteriors with uniform weights instead of applying
Eq.~\ref{eq:onestep}. At $(14, 24)$ our reproduction reads 16\% more documents
than reported. We therefore stop every method by backward induction and report
BSE-official only as a reference.

\section{Fitting the Direct Ratio}
\label{app:fit}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:fit} summarises the fit. The kernel width and penalty
minimise the held-out uLSIF criterion
\begin{equation}
J(\sigma, \lambda) = \frac{1}{2|\mathcal{H}|}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{H}} \hat r(x^h_j)^2
  - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{F}} \hat r(x^f_i),
\label{eq:cv}
\end{equation}
the empirical squared error up to a constant, averaged over five folds, where
$\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{H}$ index the held-out factual and hallucinated
documents. Folds are grouped by claim, so that the documents of one claim
never straddle folds.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Fitting the direct ratio}
\label{alg:fit}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require features $\{x^f_i\}_{i=1}^{n_f}$, $\{x^h_j\}_{j=1}^{n_h}$ with the claim of each document; grids $\Sigma$, $\Lambda$
\Ensure normalised ratio $\tilde r$; look-ahead support $\mathcal{S}$
\State standardise the features on the pooled sample
\State draw centres $c_1, \dots, c_L$ from the factual sample
\For{$(\sigma, \lambda) \in \Sigma \times \Lambda$}
  \For{each of five folds, grouped by claim}
    \State fit $\hat\alpha$ on the training part (Eq.~\ref{eq:ulsif})
    \State evaluate $J$ on the held-out part (Eq.~\ref{eq:cv})
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State $(\sigma^\star, \lambda^\star) \gets$ the pair with the smallest mean $J$
\State refit $\hat\alpha$ on all data with $(\sigma^\star, \lambda^\star)$
\State $\tilde r \gets \hat r \,/\, \frac{1}{n_h}\sum_j \hat r(x^h_j)$, clipped to $[10^{-6}, 10^{6}]$
\State $\mathcal{S} \gets \{\tilde r(x^h_j)\}_{j=1}^{n_h}$
\State \Return $\tilde r$, $\mathcal{S}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

All direct estimators use $L = 100$ centres,
$\sigma \in \{0.125, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4\}$ times the median pairwise distance,
and $\lambda \in \{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, \dots, 10\}$. Cross-validation selected,
in standardised units, $\sigma = 0.50$ and $\lambda = 0.1$ for DDRE-1D, and
$\sigma = 0.55$ and $\lambda = 0.01$ for DDRE-M. No selected value lies on the
edge of its grid. After normalisation, every direct ratio averages one over
its hallucinated training documents.

\section{Development History}
\label{app:history}

Our first estimator was a one-dimensional uLSIF ratio fitted on BSE's training
pairs. Its cross-validated configuration could not match BSE's quality on
validation data at any stopping band, so a stopping band and kernel width were
selected from 640 validation configurations. On the SelfCheckGPT held-out split
it read 20.7\% fewer documents than BSE at similar quality, but its ratio was
not normalised (mean 0.736 under the hallucinated class), and on FActScore it
read 9.4\% more documents. A multivariate ratio run under BSE's stopping band
then improved quality on Factcheck-Bench but not on two other datasets, and a
preregistered test of it on ExpertQA and BingCheck did not confirm it. These
results motivated the single-factor design of this paper. All of them, with
their preregistrations and amendments, are in the repository.
